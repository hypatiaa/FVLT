Polygon pen

One pen for every shape: the line, the digon, the triangle, the square, the pentagon and on. The dial is a polygon whose corners are parts of a turn. Reflect it in its sides and its copies tile a sphere, a plane or a hyperbolic plane, and the pen walks that tiling. The triangle is the only polygon its corners fix completely. From the square on, the polygon has k − 3 free shape dials, and they appear here as sliders. A corner can also be ideal, ∞: it sits on the circle at infinity with angle 0, and the triangle (p, q, ∞) is the torus-knot triangle. The polygon

Which surface

A corner n has an angle of 1/(2n) of a turn, the root of unity ζ2n. Reflecting the polygon in its sides tiles a surface exactly when every corner is of this kind (Poincaré's polygon theorem; Coxeter). Which surface is one exact comparison of fractions:

Rigid and free

A polygon with k corners has k sides, and closing it up is three equations (where it ends, and which way it faces). With its corners fixed, k − 3 of its sides are therefore free. A triangle has none: its corners decide its sides, by the law of cosines for angles, and each side has an exact cosine. A lune is rigid too: both sides are half a great circle. A rectangle has one free dial, its shape. A pentagon has two, a hexagon three, and so on. The free sides come back as a continuum; the triangle is the one polygon with no continuum in it at all.

On the disk, the sliders set the first k − 3 sides as L0·et, where L0 is the side of the regular polygon with the average corner, and the last three sides are solved so the polygon closes (Newton's method; the residual is shown). With all corners equal and every slider at 0, the polygon is regular and its side is exact: cosh(ℓ/2) = Re ζ2k / Im ζ4n. For the right-angled pentagon this gives cosh ℓ = the golden ratio. The rectangle's dial is its height over its width, chosen from fractions, so the plane stays exact.

The pen

Standing on a corner n, the pen sees 2n edges spaced evenly. It turns to the edge next to the one it came along, left or right as the sign says: (n − 1)/(2n) of a turn, the root of unity ζ2nn−1. Then it walks that edge. The corners it stands on go 1, 2, …, k, 1, … whatever the signs, so the turns and steps repeat with period k, and k lefts walk once round the polygon. The line has no corners: the pen steps one to the left or right.

The ideal corner and torus knots

Let a corner go to infinity: n = ∞, angle 0 of a turn, a point on the circle at infinity. Then 1/p + 1/q + 0 < 1, so the triangle (p, q, ∞) is hyperbolic (except (2, 2, ∞), which is flat). Its rotations form the free product Cp ∗ Cq: one rotation of order p at corner p, one of order q at corner q, and their product is parabolic, turning about the ideal corner. This is the group of the torus knot T(p, q) with its centre divided out: the knot group is ⟨x, y | xp = yq⟩ and xp generates the centre (Schreier). T(p, q) is a knot when p and q are coprime and a link of gcd(p, q) circles otherwise. (2, 3, ∞) is the modular group PSL(2, ℤ), and T(2, 3) is the trefoil.

The pen cannot reach an ideal corner, which is infinitely far away, so it walks the one finite side, from p to q and back. Around corner p, only every other edge is such a side, so it turns to the next one of those: (p − 2)/(2p) of a turn, ζ2pp−2, left or right as the sign says. At a corner 2 there is only one way on, so the pen goes straight and that sign is not read. These sides form a tree (the Bass–Serre tree of Cp ∗ Cq), and the pen never turns back, so every walk is a geodesic in the tree: after t steps it is exactly t edges from the start, whatever the stream. The stream is not lost; it is written down as a reduced word in Cp ∗ Cq.

For (2, 3, ∞) the word is a path in the Farey tree. Each pass through the 3-corner multiplies by R = [[1, 1], [0, 1]] for + or L = [[1, 0], [1, 1]] for −, in SL(2, ℤ), and Ra₀La₁Ra₂… is the continued fraction [a₀; a₁, a₂, …]: the run lengths of the signs read are the partial quotients of the point at infinity the pen heads toward. After a finite run the pen stands at the Farey node (a + b)/(c + d) of M = [[a, b], [c, d]], whose continued fraction is the same with the last term one larger. The page shows them, and the Farey node, exactly.

Exact, numerical, drawing

Sphere. A finite flag machine (snapping at 10⁻⁶), as in the triangle pen. Plane. Triangles exact in ℤ[ζ24]; rectangles exact as integer pairs (width units, height units); the line exact in ℤ. Disk. SU(1,1) products; the view follows the pen; one tiling patch per corner. Triangles and regular polygons have exact sides; the other polygons are solved numerically.
