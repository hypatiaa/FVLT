Rosette space-time (rows = ticks, columns = rings)

Each column is one ring and ignores its neighbours: a clockwork. Ring n touches the axis every n ticks; odd rings always on the +ray (blue), even rings alternating −ray (orange) and +ray (blue), so the coloured column repeats every n ticks for odd n and every 2n ticks for even n.

Rule 30

Each cell reads its two neighbours: new = left XOR (centre OR right). The marked column is the centre column, the sequence used as random bits.

Entropy (FVLT)

One ring

Each ring n is on the axis with probability exactly 1/n over its readout period n: it touches the axis at the ticks s that are multiples of n, one tick in every n. The hand itself, and the ray it touches (the colour), repeat every n ticks for odd n and every 2n ticks for even n: at s = kn the ray is + when n is odd or k is even, and − when n is even and k is odd. The on/off readout used below has period n.

The readout law

Let s run uniformly over one full period L = lcm(1..W) of the readout. The on-set D = Div(s) ∩ [1, W] is in bijection with the truncated valuation box: for each prime p ≤ W let ap be the largest a with pa ≤ W, and ep = min(vp(s), ap). By the Chinese remainder theorem the ep are independent, with P(ep = k) = p−k(1 − 1/p) for k < ap and p−ap for k = ap. So HW = Σp≤W H(ep), and the number of distinct on-sets is ∏(ap + 1). This is the automaton's counterpart of the divisor gas: each prime is an independent valuation "spin".

Two shells

The instrument phasor is eiπ·k·s/N. Ring 3 of this automaton is a shell-3 clock: its hand visits 1, ω, ω2 (ω = e2πi/3). Ring 2 is a shell-2 clock: it lands every 2 ticks, alternating the +ray and the −ray. A landing is the hand on the real axis. The two sets below are separate parameters: each has its own drive and its own dial, and nothing is shared between them.

Set A · shell N = 3 · the divisor-lattice walk

Golay gauge (bus #2041/#2043/#2056). The atoms of Div(30030) split into the triples A = (2, 3, 5) and B = (7, 11, 13), each oriented as a cycle, 2→3→5→2 and 7→11→13→7 (a declared convention). A state is a divisor with one atom from each triple, 9 states, for example 14 = 2·7. A move changes one triple's atom; its displacement is +1 or −1 in F3 (forward or backward along the cycle), in coordinate x (triple A) or y (triple B). So d ∈ F32, S ∈ F32, and the area Q ∈ F3. Frame update: (S, Q) → (S + d, Q + ½·S∧d), with ½ = 2 in F3 and S∧d = Sxdy − Sydx (mod 3).
Mirror pair k1/k2: the ω dial draws ωQ, the ω̄ dial draws the conjugate ω−Q; Q is the same integer for both. Reversing the orientation of both cycles leaves Q unchanged (S∧d is bilinear, and both S and d change sign); reversing one cycle sends Q to −Q, which is the ω̄ picture.

The two automata

Rosette: cell n holds an integer phase e in 0..2n−1 (the hand points at the root of unity ζ2ne); each tick e → e + (n − 1) mod 2n; read: e = 0 or e = n, which happens exactly when n divides s. No cell looks at another.
Rule 30: cell x holds one bit; each tick it becomes left XOR (centre OR right); read: the centre cell. Every cell depends on its neighbours.
The rosette is perfectly ordered and every residue and divisibility frequency comes out exactly; Rule 30 looks random. Both are deterministic. Statistics of single values cannot tell order from randomness; how a value depends on the ones before it can. The visible difference here is two-dimensional: each rosette cell ignores its neighbours, each Rule 30 cell depends on them.
Scope: an illustration and evidence only; nothing on this page proves anything about Rule 30.
