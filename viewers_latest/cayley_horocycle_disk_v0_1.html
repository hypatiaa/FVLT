Cayley Horocycle Disk

v0.1 · 2026-11-01 · The Cayley (geometric) viewer. It is a separate viewer from the Farey Horocycle Disk, which is the FVLT viewer in the exponential lift; neither succeeds the other. This one shows the same objects with the same certified readout, in the chart w = (z − i)/(z + i), where distances are true and every point and circle is an exact rational. The Riemann hypothesis seen in the Poincaré disk, through two classical equivalent forms: Franel–Landau and Zagier. The question came from saphir. The results are classical and belong to the people named below.

Registers.
  • Exact. Every value is a BigInt rational: the disk geometry, the Farey data, the discrepancy and the counts.
  • Certified. Irrational values (√ and π) are given as a pair of rationals that are guaranteed to contain them. Square roots come from integer square roots, and π from Machin's series with every rounding error counted.
  • Drawing. Values become decimals only when they are turned into SVG coordinates or canvas pixels. In this chart every circle, kiss and arc end is an exact rational point, or the image of one, before it is rounded to a pixel. Nothing computed is ever read back from a pixel.
There is no floating-point arithmetic in any result on this page.

1 · The disk

The map w = (z − i)/(z + i) sends the upper half-plane onto the disk. The centre 0 is z = i, and ∞ goes to w = 1. Each fraction p/q lands on the circle at an exact rational point, with no angle involved:

w(p/q) = (p − iq)/(p + iq) = ( (p² − q²) + (−2pq) i ) / (p² + q²)

That is a Pythagorean point, the unit Gaussian rational of p + iq. Every circle on this page has an exact rational centre and an exact rational radius, or an exact rational radius².

  • Grey arcs are the Farey lines, joining a/b and c/d whenever |ad − bc| = 1. Each has a rational centre (w₁ + w₂)/(1 + w₁·w₂), and its radius² is |centre|² − 1.
  • Blue circles are the Ford circles. The one at p/q has centre (p² − q², −2pq)/(1 + p² + q²) and radius 1/(1 + p² + q²).
  • The green circle is the horocycle Im z = 1/H. Its centre is 1/(H + 1) and its radius H/(H + 1), both exact. It touches the rim at the cusp w = 1. It crosses the Ford circle at p/q exactly when q² < H, which is an integer comparison. When q² = H it touches that circle (shown dashed).
  • Dots are the kisses. Farey neighbours a/q and c/s touch at z = (aq + cs)/m + i/m, where m = q² + s² (the Landau Ford-tangency lemma). In the disk that is the exact rational point w = (X² + Y² − 1, −2X)/(X² + (Y + 1)²). The horocycle with H = m passes through it, and those kisses are ringed.

The companion FVLT viewer (Farey Horocycle Disk, exponential lift) shows the same objects with the cusp at the centre, fractions at roots of unity ζqp and kisses at e−2π/m·ζmaq+cs. The two are different charts of one picture: here a fraction is a Pythagorean point and a horocycle has rational radius; there a fraction is a root of unity and a horocycle has radius e−2π/H.

1a · Animation: the horocycle swelling from the cusp

In the Cayley disk, the horocycle Im z = 1/H is the green circle with centre 1/(H + 1) and radius H/(H + 1). It always touches the rim at the cusp w = 1. As H grows (y = 1/H → 0), it swells from that rim point until it nearly fills the disk.

  • Orange discs are the cusp regions {Im γz > Y}: one disc at each p/q, with diameter 1/(q²Y) in the half-plane. In the disk the disc at x is the circle tangent to the rim at w(x) with radius d/(d + 1 + x²), d = 1/(q²Y). When Y = 1 they are exactly the Ford circles. The horocycle reaches the disc at p/q once q²Y < H.
  • Thick orange arcs are the parts of the green circle inside those discs. Their total share of the circle is h, the closed form from section 3.
  • Green gaps are where the horocycle runs between discs. Black dots are kisses (Y = 1); when H = q² + s² with gcd(q, s) = 1 the circle passes through the kisses of that m, and they are ringed.
  • As the circle swells, more arcs appear, and h approaches the cusp's share of the area, 3/(πY). Only one period's worth of translates, |x| ≤ 8, is drawn; the rest crowd into the cusp at w = 1.

Every frame uses an integer H, and its readout is certified exactly as in section 3. The horocycle's centre and radius are exact rationals. The circles and arcs are drawing register.

The trace shows log10|E| against log10 H, building up as the animation plays. The dashed lines have slopes −1/2 and −3/4. Points where the sign of E is not certified are left out.

2 · Franel–Landau: how evenly the Farey fractions sit

Let r1 < … < rm be the Farey fractions of order n in (0, 1], so m = φ(1) + … + φ(n). The discrepancy is

D(n) = Σv | rv − v/m |

It is an exact rational. Franel and Landau (1924): RH holds if and only if D(n) = O(n1/2 + ε) for every ε > 0. The ratio D(n)/√n is certified from the exact rational D(n)²/n using integer square roots.

The chart shows D(n)/√n for n = 1 … 400, as the certified lower bound, which agrees with the upper bound to 40 digits. A finite chart cannot decide RH.

3 · Zagier: how evenly a long horocycle fills the surface

Fold the disk by PSL(2, ℤ), the trefoil group (2, 3, ∞). The result is the modular surface, which has area π/3. Take the horocycle at height y = 1/H for an integer H, and the cusp {Im z > Y} for an integer Y ≥ 1. The cusp's share of the area is 3/(πY).

The horocycle's share inside the cusp is built from the Ford circles, one denominator c at a time:

h = Σc² Y < H 2 φ(c) √((H − c²Y) Y) / (c H Y)

Every √ in it is the square root of an integer. The error is E = h − 3/(πY). Euler's φ enters through the Farey denominators, and ζ through Σ φ(c)/cs = ζ(s − 1)/ζ(s).

Zagier (1981): for smooth test functions, RH holds if and only if the error is O(y3/4 − ε). Unconditionally it is O(y1/2) (Sarnak, 1981). This cusp region has a sharp edge, so it is not a smooth test function. This section shows the mechanism; it does not test RH.

Where “always through zero” fits

The waves of the hyperbolic plane go like ys. On the line s = ½ + it they only turn and never grow or shrink, and this line is the bottom of the spectrum, ¼ + t². On the modular surface, the factor that measures how much of a wave comes back out of the cusp is

φ(s) = √π · Γ(s − ½) · ζ(2s − 1) / (Γ(s) · ζ(2s))

(Selberg). The zeros of ζ are therefore poles of this factor. RH says they sit where the waves keep their size. The horocycle in section 3 is how those waves are measured: the rate at which it fills the surface is governed by them.

In this chart the horocycle does not go around the centre: it is a circle through the cusp w = 1. Averaging a function along it is still the constant term ys + φ(s)·y1−s of the Eisenstein series (Zagier's method). In the companion FVLT viewer the same horocycle is the circle |w| = e−2πy around the centre, where that average is the w0 coefficient.
